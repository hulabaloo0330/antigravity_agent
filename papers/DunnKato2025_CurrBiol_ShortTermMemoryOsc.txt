Working memory by distributed neural oscillators in a simple nervous system 13 5 2025 2024.08.11.607402 bioRxiv 10.1101/2024.08.11.607402 PMC11326443 39149308 SUMMARY Short-term memory allows an animal to gather sensory evidence over time, integrate it with evolving internal state, and make informed decisions about when and how to act at a later time. The implementation of short-term memory by brains remains an outstanding mystery. Simpler animals such as the microscopic nematode  C. elegans  present the opportunity to understand, at a deep mechanistic level, how cognitive functions such as short-term memory are implemented by biological neural networks. We first establish that  C. elegans  can remember recent sensory experience to make discrete, informed turns for chemotaxis. Then, using a closed-loop virtual-reality system combining whole-brain imaging and targeted optogenetic neural perturbation, we find that this memory is held in the relative phase of the distributed oscillations of two groups of many neurons. One oscillatory neural complex drives the sequence of well-defined behavioral command states of the animal, and the other oscillatory neural complex drives large swings of the animal’s head, during forward crawling. However, during reverse crawling, the headswing oscillatory complex, in coordination with the command state complex, serves as a phase-based memory system. We propose that the implementation of a short-term memory system via the internalization of motor oscillations could represent the evolutionary origin of flexible internal neural network processing, i.e. thought, and a foundation of higher cognition. eTOC Blurb: Using closed-loop optogenetics with simultaneous whole-brain imaging, Dunn et al, discover a short-term memory system in  C. elegans  implemented by the interaction of two distributed neural oscillator complexes, used to make deferred actions based on prior sensory evidence during foraging locomotion, possibly representing a primordial acquisition of internal cognitive function. Such a memory system allows for deferred, contingent actions, which may serve the animal better than reflex actions by allowing for more sophisticated behavior or cognitive processing. It is unknown how nervous systems first acquired this ability. Distributed brain oscillations and their interactions have long been hypothesized to be a building block of cognitive function in complex-brained animals. 1  Considerable theoretical work has been devoted to understanding how these observed phenomena might implement various cognitive functions, including short-term memory. 2 , 3  Some human cognitive functions are hypothesized to depend on interactions between distributed oscillations of different frequencies, such as phase-amplitude coupling, a form of cross-frequency coupling (CFC), in hippocampal neurons, 4  or information routing through phase coherence in cortex (CTC). 5  However, experimental establishment of causal roles and mechanistic understanding has been elusive, due at least in part to the sheer complexity of mammalian brains and the challenge of observing these neural networks at a high level of sampling density and comprehensiveness. Perturbative studies, while growing in sophistication, have yielded only modest abilities to control global network dynamics. 6 By contrast, in simpler-brained animals with vastly lower neuron counts, oscillators serving to produce repetitive bodily movement, i.e. central pattern generators, have been closely studied and systematically dissected, yielding fundamental mechanistic insight in the production of adaptive yet robust rhythmic motor patterns, implemented using variable, noisy, biological components. 7  We wondered whether there is a connection between the motor oscillators of simpler, primordial animals and the distributed oscillations observed in the vastly more complex brains of mammals, separated by more than 400 million years of evolution. 8 Unlike the generation of motor oscillations, cognitive functions such as sensory-driven decision making have not been elucidated with the same level of mechanistic detail in simple animals, 9  due in part to the difficulty of training simple animals to perform controlled behavioral tasks, the historically dominant approach which allows for trial averaging to overcome the ubiquitous trial-to-trial variability of neural activity. 10 , 11  Recent work has begun to dissect naturalistic decision making behaviors in simpler model organisms, leveraging advances in massively parallel dense neural activity recording methods. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 The 1 mm long nematode  C. elegans  crawls on its right or left side when on a flat surface. During foraging, the worm crawls in a straight or gently curving forward direction and punctuates bouts of forward crawling with discrete reversal-then-turn reorientation maneuvers, known as pirouettes, either in the dorsal or ventral direction ( Figure 1A ). 17  The choice of this reorientation direction has traditionally been described as an unregulated random process, akin to the direction-randomizing tumbles of a bacterium performing “biased-random-walk” chemotaxis. 17  However, previous studies have shown that worm olfactory neurons reliably signal on surprisingly fast timescales, 18 , 19  which are approximately matched to the frequency of head movements during forward crawling. Furthermore, mutations which slow temporal dynamics of olfactory receptors drastically disrupt chemotaxis, suggesting worms can rapidly integrate sensory information with instantaneous body posture. 18 Here, we construct a closed-loop virtual olfactory environment and show that worms integrate olfactory sensory dynamics with instantaneous body posture to execute directed turns, after a variable intervening period of tens of seconds, suggesting the existence of a simple memory system. By monitoring neural activity across the whole brain from olfactory neurons receiving external input to motor neurons driving muscle output, we find that actionable short-term memory is implemented by the phasic interaction of two distributed dynamical complexes associated with motor control. RESULTS C. elegans executes directed, sensory-guided reorientations while foraging We engineered a closed-loop virtual stimulus system to emulate a lateralized (i.e. spatially directional in the worm’s frame of reference) odor signal by delivering optogenetic stimulation timed to dorsal or ventral body postures of the worm while crawling on an agar surface, then monitoring the worm’s subsequent reversal-then-turn behavior. In worms foraging on a 2D agar surface, we optogenetically stimulated the AWA olfactory sensory neuron timed with either dorsal or ventral head bends to mimic the natural temporal oscillation in odor concentrations at the worm’s nose tip expected during sinusoidal crawling on a chemical gradient ( Figure 1A ). 18 , 19  AWA is necessary for chemotaxis to certain attractive bacterial metabolites, 20  and can fire bursts of all-or-none action potentials at up to 10 Hz mediated by calcium conductances. 19  In the absence of explicit sensory cues, when crawling on an agar surface, worm post-reversal turns are biased 60–75% towards their ventral side. 21  After posture-timed optogenetic stimulation during bouts of forward crawling, individuals showed a strong preference for finishing the following reversal bout with a turn in the stimulation-biased direction before resuming forward locomotion ( Figure 1B ). This observation suggests two key features of this sensory-directed behavior. First, to attribute the stimulus to a particular spatial direction, the animal needs to integrate olfactory sensation with proprioceptive signals or efference signals of motor commands for head swings. Second, to turn in a favorable direction after an intervening reversal bout, intuitively, the animal must employ some form of short-term memory ( Figure 1C ). Using whole-brain neuron-resolution imaging, we sought to find the locus of this short-term memory. Distributed dynamical complexes encode motor command state and head bending Studies across the animal kingdom have established a universal phenomenon that high-level (organismal) motor commands are encoded in the low-dimensional state space of broadly distributed neural dynamics. 9 , 13 , 15 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25  We asked whether lower-level behavioral commands may also be encoded across many neurons, such as the sinusoidal movement of head swings in  C. elegans  exhibited during forward locomotion. Previous studies have observed several neurons which correlate with dorsal or ventral head bending; however, to what extent these signals reflect head musculature proprioception, motor commands, causal decision processes, or any combination thereof, is unresolved. 22 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 To look for neurons encoding headswing commands while avoiding the potential confound of proprioceptive encoding, we restrained worms in microfluidic chips that enable high-quality volumetric whole-brain calcium imaging at cellular resolution using a pan-neuronal nuclear-localized calcium sensor ( Figure 2A , B ). In all trials, we observed cycles of the fictive command state sequence (forward-backward-turn) widely distributed across many neurons, consistent with prior studies. 22 , 29  For convenience, we define command states (and corresponding fictive neural network states): forward (FWD), reversal (REV), and dorsal or ventral (DOR, VEN) turn (TURN). As reported previously, principal component analysis (PCA) reveals that the first principal component of whole brain recordings reliably encodes the command state sequence ( Figure 2C , S2 ). 22  We refer to the set of neurons which exhibit these shared dynamics as the  command state complex , and the first principal component of these dynamics as the  command state projection . To assess the extent to which the command state projection reflects the activity of individual neurons in the command state complex, we computed the Pearson correlation coefficient between each neuronal trace and the command state projection ( Figure 2D ). We used this approach rather than examining PCA loadings to account for neuron-specific differences in dynamic range (see Methods). Most neurons exhibiting strong correlations with the command state projection have previously been implicated in modulating forward or reverse locomotion (RIM, RIB, AIB, 30  RID, 31  AVA, AVE, AVB, 32  RME 33 ). Additionally, the OLQ and URY neuron classes were found to have strong positive correlation to the command state projection, for which there is prior evidence, though weaker, of involvement in locomotory behavior. 29  The command state complex is dominated by two core bistable groups; neurons which correlate with forward locomotion counterposed with neurons which correlate with reverse locomotion ( Figure 2G ). Since the command state signal dominates the variance of brain-wide neuronal activity, we then performed PCA on the residuals of the command state projection, while restricting time series data to periods of fictive forward locomotion only, which yielded a second strong, stereotyped network oscillation ( Figure 2E ). We found this faster oscillating component to be distributed across 12+ neurons in the head of the animal that are variously implicated in motor control of the head muscles, sensorimotor integration, and proprioception ( Figures 2F ,  2G ,  S1A ). 26 , 27 , 30  The neuron class we observed with highest correlation to this faster oscillating component was SMDD, a pair of cholinergic sensorimotor neurons which depolarize in response to muscle stretch and can influence locomotory bearing. 26 , 27  The corresponding contralateral sensorimotor neuron class SMDV showed a large negative correlation. Previous studies have shown SMDV reliably correlates with ventral head curvature during forward crawling and ventral post-reversal turns, and SMDD correlates with dorsal turns ( Figure S1B , C ). 22 , 26 , 28  Their anti-phasic coupling during FWD suggests their activity corresponds to fictive dorsal-ventral head swings consistent with sinusoidal crawling during the forward command state. We name this component the  headswing projection  and the set of neurons exhibiting these shared dynamics the  headswing complex . Other neurons in the headswing complex include the motor neurons SAAV and RIV showed high correlation with the headswing projection despite relatively weak connectivity to other headswing complex neurons ( Figure 2G ). 30 , 34 , 35  The RIA nuclear calcium signal did not emerge as a strong correlate to either complex, though based on prior studies, we would expect that RIA subcompartment calcium dynamics in the nrV and nrD regions of its nerve ring neurite would exhibit strong negative and positive correlations to the headswing projection respectively. 26  Inhibition or ablation of headswing complex neurons SMDV, SMDD, RMDD/RMDV, RIV, and SAAV has been shown to suppress head bending or pirouette sequences. 26 , 28 , 30 , 34  Several neuron classes multiplex, i.e. participate in both complexes, such as RME, RMDD, RMDV, AVE and AIB. The synaptic connectivity among headswing complex neurons does not obviously define distinct neural sub-networks ( Figure S3 ). While TURN trajectories of different directions are mixed together in a single bundle when plotted in command state projection phase space ( Figure S2A ), they strongly split when plotted in joint command/headswing state space ( Figure 2H ,  2I ). By plotting the command state projection against the derivative of the headswing projection ( Figure 2I ), we can observe a relationship between the onset timing, or phase of the post-reversal turn and the turn identity. We asked if there was a relationship between the oscillation periods of the two projections. Previous studies have observed that chronic inhibition of SMDV or optogenetic activation of SAAV and SAAD (which was not identifiable in our recordings), or RIV, can impact reversal timing, 28 , 34 , 36  suggesting command state and headswing complexes may interact. Interestingly, we found that distribution of the duration of FWD/REV epochs of the command state projection was enriched at integer multiples of the headswing projection periods ( Figure 2I ,  Figure 2J ). This coupling of internal dynamics is consistent with the nesting of headswings within forward crawling bouts previously reported. 28  The persistence of an interaction across multiple fictive headswing periods suggested to us a potential substrate for maintaining a stable value, i.e. a memory, via the phase of the headswing projection oscillation, that could be leveraged for executing deferred sensory-guided decisions. To test this hypothesis, we examined neural network dynamics while evoking directed TURNs. Decoding deferred, sensory-driven action in immobilized worms We next combined closed-loop perturbative optogenetic stimulation with whole-brain imaging ( Figure 3A - C ), recapitulating our virtual olfactory setup in immobilized worms. Each 16–24 minute recording (39 worms) captured 10–30 FWD-REV-TURN sequences. Within a trial, repeated AWA stimulations of ~4–8s were delivered at a particular phase of the headswing projection oscillation, inferred from the depolarization of SMDV or SMDD, during the FWD command state, inferred from the depolarization of RID or hyperpolarization of AVA ( Figure 3C ,  D ). Stimulation of AWA shifted the distribution of times until the next REV ( Figure 3E ,  F ), sometimes eliciting immediate reversals. We observed a secondary bump in the shifted distribution of time-to-REV for AWA stimulations, at ~20s, which roughly corresponds to one typical period of fictive headswing oscillation under immobilization ( Figure 2G ,  3D ), suggesting that we may be observing the system as it maintains a sensory-evoked memory trace over the course of one of more fictive reversal headswing oscillations. We evaluated the direction of the TURN produced after the intervening REV state, about 30 to 90 seconds in the future ( Figure 3G ), and found that our stimulations could bias the DOR/VEN ratio in both directions, depending on the value of the estimated headswing phase at the time of last stimulation before a reversal ( Figure 3H ). Next, we trained a decoder on individual stimulation trials (see Methods) to predict TURN direction using activity leading up to the prior REV. We found that the dorsal/ventral identity of fictive post-reversal turns could be predicted by evoked AWA neural responses when combined with phase information of headswing projection at the time of stimulation ( Figure 3I ), consistent with a causal effect of stimulation to bias turn choice ( Figure 3H ). Prediction improved by including headswing state at REV onset ( Figure 3J ), suggesting a post-stimulation role for headswing state in informing the deferred turn decision. Including features of individual headswing complex neuronal activity alongside the headswing projection further increased prediction performance ( Figure 3J ), possibly suggesting that linear projection of headswing complex neurons may not fully capture the network dynamics underlying the sensation-to-action process. While the ability to predict future TURN direction >30 s in the future from neural activity is surprising, the inability to predict future TURNs with complete accuracy may reflect variable stimulus saliency or missing measurement of causal latent factors. Finally, we asked whether our model preferentially correctly predicted TURN direction after immediate REV initiation, versus TURNs after delayed REVs, so we reproduced  Figure 3E  restricted to successfully predicted trials. We found the distribution of reversal initiation delay following stimulation to be broad, with only 19% of reversals occurring within 5 s of stimulation, and 44% of reversals >20 s ( Figure S4A ). The ability to predict deferred fictive turn decisions from prior neural activity in an immobilized, paralyzed setup demonstrates the existence of an internal short-term memory. We next tested whether this memory ability occurred when nesting was exhibited by the command state and headswing projections, by repeating the analysis from  Figure 2J  using only trials correctly predicted (as in  Figure 3J ); nesting was still observed ( Figure S1D ). Given the evidence of interaction between headswing projection oscillation and command state projection oscillations, we sought a closer study of oscillatory headswing dynamics present during the intervening reversal period. Internalized headswing oscillation phase is set after REV onset During reverse locomotion, the head oscillations present during forward crawling are largely suppressed – the animal’s head trails behind the body. 37  We found that during REV epochs in paralyzed worms, headswing projection dynamics continue to display oscillatory behavior; however, the magnitude is typically attenuated and the regularity of the oscillation waveform appears reduced ( Figures 2E ,  3D ,  S2D ,  E ). We refer to these dynamics as  internalized  because they persist in the absence of both sensory input (i.e. in completely immobilized worms) and evident motor behavioral output (i.e. during reversal/REV). To compare headswing oscillation amplitude, frequency, and phase during FWD and the following REV epoch, we fit single sinusoids to smoothed derivatives of the headswing projection as well as SMDV, SMDD, and RIV, which participate in the headswing complex ( Figure 4A ,  Figure S5 ). These neurons were selected because of their high correlation to head bending, 26  and their reliably high correlation to the headswing projection ( Figure 2D ). Toward the end of a reversal epoch, the magnitude of the headswing projection oscillation typically increases over the course of 1–2 oscillations, exhibited in SMDD/V as well as other neurons ( Figure 4A ). We observed statistically significant correlations between amplitude and frequency between headswing during FWD and REV ( Figure 4B , C ), suggesting they may be stable system properties independent of actual movement production, but we found no correlation between phase from FWD to the subsequent REV, suggesting a change of phase ( Figure 4D ). Despite the irregularity and reduced magnitude of the headswing projection during the initial segment of a REV epoch, we wondered if the headswing complex maintained an ability to convey information in its oscillatory activity until the time of REV termination. We again fit sinusoids to activity during REV; however, this time we separately fit on the first and second halves of REV ( Figure 4E ) and compared fit parameters. We found small but statistically significant correlations in amplitude, frequency, and phase between early and late REV, suggesting that the internalized headswing oscillation can be somewhat stable over the course of a REV epoch ( Figure 4F – H ). Returning to headswing dynamics during FWD or REV segments, we found that the phase of our sinusoid fits stratified turn choice, particularly during REV ( Figure 4J , K ). Therefore, we conclude that the phase of the headswing complex is clustered to one of two intervals [0, π] or [π, 2π] when internalized, thereby storing sensory information that guides the intent of future turns. The data suggest that the headswing complex oscillation decouples from motor output during reversals but does not disappear, and it recouples leading up to a reversal termination in order to effect the post-reversal turn. Headswing complex neurons terminate the REV command state While the head projection oscillation is distributed across many neurons and is implicated in the memory process, it is possible that the memory trace that is causal for choice of future action is limited to a subset of neurons or even one neuron. To probe the sufficiency of individual neurons in holding a sensory memory for driving turn identity, we selectively stimulated headswing complex neurons, SMDD, SMDV, RIA, and RIV. We used a digital micromirror device to spatially restrict our laser stimulation to selectively stimulate particular neurons, timing stimulations to occur during fictive reversals ( Figure 5A ,  B ). Surprisingly, selective optogenetic stimulation, though demonstrably depolarizing the neurons (SMDV shown in  Figure 5B , L ), did not bias the subsequent TURN direction ( Figure 5L ,  Figure S6A ). This suggests that the activity of SMDD/SMDV neurons, while clearly signaling TURN identity at the time of the turn, are not the locus of the causal memory trace. Unexpectedly, despite the inability to bias turn direction, stimulation of either SMD neuron class often elicited immediate reversal terminations ( Figure 5C ,  D ,  H ). We next turned to the head neurons RIA and RIV. We found that spatially localized optogenetic stimulations of ventral or dorsal subcompartments of RIA nerve ring neurite (nrV, nrD), as well as RIV, also often immediately terminated reversals ( Figure 5E – G ). Command state dynamics serve as a reference clock for headswing oscillations How does the system “know” when to recouple the internalized headswing oscillation and terminate a reversal to produce the correct turn direction? To store a persistent value (i.e. memory) reliably using the phase of an oscillator over several oscillation periods, a reference clock with a predictable relationship to the oscillator is needed. We hypothesized that the ability of certain headswing neurons to immediately terminate fictive reversals is proprioceptive in nature; they signal the successful execution of physical bending that produces reverse movement. We surmised that as the command state projection evolves in time, the system becomes permissive for proprioception-driven reversal termination. We found that the capability of RIA subcompartments to terminate fictive reversals was gated by the instantaneous activity of the command state projection at the time of stimulation on a per-trial basis ( Figure 5I ,  J ). Furthermore, individual neuron predictivity was positively correlated with command state projection loading ( Fig. 5I ,  J ). We fit an exponential to the command state projection during REV ( Figure 5K ,  Figure S6B ) and found that the time constant of command state projection ramping is correlated with reversal duration, the timescale over which the memory must be maintained, suggesting that the command complex may function as a reliable time reference. The decay in activity of positive loading neurons such as the mechanosensory neuron OLQ, the oxygen sensing neuron URX, the interneuron AIB or the ramping of neurons with negative loading such as RME, could potentially function as behavioral timers in conjunction with other sensory or motor roles previously proposed ( Figure S6B ). 38 , 39 , 40  Command state complex neurons OLQV and RME, and URX, individually show high accuracy for predicting immediate reversal terminations, i.e. immediate TURN initiations ( Figure 5I ), suggesting they may play a role in integration of dynamics exhibited by the two complexes. Notably, AVA, the command state complex neuron that signals body motor neurons to execute sinusoidal reversal crawling motor patterns, was not strongly predictive of the reversal termination, substantiating its role as a command state output gate rather than a lone driver of the decision to initiate reversals. 22 Focusing next on stimulations that elicited a TURN by terminating REV, we found that the activity level of the headswing projection, as well as the activity of individual neurons with high headswing loadings, was predictive of the TURN direction ( Figure 5M ). Using our decoding framework, we were unable to find neurons whose static, monotonic, or differential activity levels were predictive of turn identity. We suspect the non-monotonic time course of predictivity is a consequence of oscillatory dynamics in these neurons; the headswing projection captures both DOR and VEN depolarizations, whereas individual headswing complex neurons display strongly turn-direction-dependent differential activity only during half of their oscillation cycle ( Figure 5M . The simultaneous ramping of the command state complex and period nesting with the headswing complex ( Figure 2J ) may allow the animal to balance two functions: drive to terminate reversal and turn in the correct direction. A dual half-center oscillator model reproduces nested projection dynamics To give concreteness to our proposed mechanism based on coupled neural oscillators, we sought to develop a parsimonious computational model that could recapitulate the observed period nesting phenomenon and also capture qualitative features of the observed time series of the neural complexes. The emergence of spontaneous, robust oscillations, without the need for fine tuning, from two cross-inhibiting neurons or groups of neurons has been well modeled by the half-center oscillator (HCO) ( Figure 6A ). 41 , 42  We modeled command state and headswing complexes as two separate HCOs ( Figure 6B , see Methods), HCO(slow) and HCO(fast) respectively, incorporating an interaction term as an input current from HCO(fast) to HCO(slow). We observed that our HCO model readily produces both saturating (square-like) and non-saturating (sinusoid) push-pull oscillatory dynamics reminiscent of the waveforms of the command state projection and headswing projection ( Figure 2F ,  G ). When we injected random current into a unit, representing fluctuations from sensory input and other internal processes, the model produced a nested distribution of state durations resembling the experimental distribution ( Figure 6C  versus  2J ). DISCUSSION We found that worms are capable of producing directed turns by gathering sensory evidence and integrating it with body posture, revising a longstanding notion that foraging reorientations are undirected. Our data suggest that worms can navigate odor environments based on the following mechanism: (a) sensory input is received during crawling sinusoidal head swings and integrated with body posture to form sensory memory, (b) headswing and command state dynamics are coordinated, and headswing phase is set to the interval [0, π] or [π, 2π], (c) during reversal, a ramping process in the command state complex gates reversal termination, until (d) a proprioceptive signal indicates successful movement execution, terminating REV, and finally (e) a directed turn is produced based on whether the headswing complex is in DOR or VEN associated phase at the time of headswing recoupling. We found that five different neuron classes/subcompartments within the headswing complex can terminate reversals yet do not bias turn direction, providing evidence that the encoding of turn direction intent in the interaction of distributed dynamical complexes represents a behaviorally causal memory. There are many aspects of this process that remain unelucidated and merit further investigation. Several salient questions come to mind. How is the sensory signal integrated with proprioceptive signals? How is this integrated signal transferred to a phase resetting of the headswing dynamics? It is possible that there are other intermediate carriers of the memory trace– e.g. the activity of single premotor neurons, or secondary molecular carriers such as neuropeptides not observable using our current system. Neuron-specific knockouts of signaling molecules, as well as novel biosignal imaging methods, may yield other loci of causal memory. Which neurons in the two complexes are necessary, and which are dispensable, for maintaining the memory? Future experiments could study the effects of stimulating various sets of headswing neurons. Finally, can the memory, once established, be combined with other information available to the system, suggesting the existence of a flexible working memory system? We also know that the natural head movement of the animal is not limited to oscillation in one plane; the animal often displays two-axis control of its head, e.g. lifting its head up off of the crawling surface, consistent with fourfold anatomical symmetry of much of the neuromuscular apparatus in the head. We surmise that the one-bit short-term memory of the worm we have uncovered is only part of the cognitive repertoire of this animal. A basis model for C. elegans neurodynamics The dynamical model we present in  Figure 6  supports a general architectural hypothesis: worm brain activity is composed of multiple loosely coupled dynamical complexes (e.g. HCOs) whose coordination implements cognitive functions such as short-term memory and supports purposeful behavior. The model may provide a starting point for building models of more sophisticated cognitive function. Features such as additional neural subnetworks or overlaid neuropeptide signaling could be incorporated to capture the cognitive processes enabling more of the worm’s behavioral repertoire. Further experiments may inform specific aspects of the model, such as the particular form of interaction term, and may make predictions about the use of particular microcircuit motifs. 43 A primordial short-term memory Our experiments suggest that short-term memory is implemented by the maintenance of the relative phase of two dynamical complexes, each distributed across many neurons. This mechanism was profoundly unexpected in a nervous system of such low neuron count. The potential evolutionary conservation of the functional use of distributed neural oscillations across hundreds of million years motivates the study of simple, tractable model neuroscientific systems for understanding the basis of human cognition. Perhaps even more profoundly, this study led us to a hypothesis of the evolution of early cognition. We surmise that the original function of the head neural oscillator system was to produce physical movement, and the motor-decoupled mode of operation was recruited later to support the function of short-term memory, thereby endowing the animal with the ability to perform deferred sensory-guided action selection. The co-opting of a motor-effector neural system to subserve a disembodied cognitive function may bear analogy to the emerging interpretation of hippocampal dynamics as a basis for contingent planning / imagination in addition to their role in ongoing movement. 44  We propose that the repurposing of motor command dynamics to hold information for deferred contingent actions may represent the evolutionary expansion of cognitive repertoire from reflex actions to actions guided by internal neural processing, i.e. the origin of thought itself. METHOD DETAILS Behavior imaging and quantification Worms were prepared 24 hours in advance of imaging: L4 worms were picked onto fresh NGM agar plates with an OP50 lawn. Immediately before imaging, worms were picked onto a fresh NGM agar plate without food, into a drop of M9. Finally, the worm was aspirated onto a 55mm NGM agar plate without food, with care taken to re-aspirate excess M9. Worms were imaged while crawling on the surface of the agar without lid for 1–2 recordings of 12 minutes each. Worms were tracked over the course of an experiment with the microscope’s motorized stage centered on the worm’s centroid. For experiments involving optogenetics, care was taken to reduce activation of the optogenetic channel from brightfield illumination light. About 24 hours prior to imaging, 80ul of 100uM all-trans retinal in M9 was added to the bacteria lawn of L4s. Worms were then placed in foil at 20C until imaging. During an imaging session, worms were partially covered if not being immediately prepared for an experiment. Optogenetic illumination was made in widefield mode with the LED3 set to 100%. Worms were left in tetramisole for 10 minutes prior to being aspirated into the microfluidic channel. Worms were imaged for 16–24 minutes. Data were acquired on an inverted spinning disk microscope (Yokogawa W1-SoRa) set up on a DMI8 inverted microscope stand (Leica Microsystems) equipped with a Kinetix sCMOS camera (Telydyne Photometrics) and a Versalase Laser combiner (Vortran). Roughly half of recordings were acquired at 2×2 camera binning, the rest without binning. The microscope objective lens was 40× 1.25NA WI. Sample volumetric scans were performed using a piezo stage (ASI) with 10–12 z-planes with z-spacing 2.5–3um. In select recordings, 4 z-planes with z-spacing 3um were used to measure neurons in lateralized anterior, lateral, dorsal, and ventral ganglia at higher temporal resolution under equivalent optical conditions. Prior to acquiring videos for calcium timeseries, a reference high-resolution structural image was acquired using 40×1um z-planes under 4 different optical conditions to measure NeuroPAL fluorescence. 48  Exposures ranged from 30ms - 80ms under 100 – 200uW/mm2 illumination to minimize photobleaching and optogenetics/fluorescence crosstalk. For experiments involving optogenetics, care was taken to reduce activation of the optogenetic channel via illumination for measuring GCaMP fluorescence. About 24 hours prior to imaging, 80ul of 1uM ATR in M9 was added to the bacterial lawn of L4s. Worms were then placed in foil at 20 C until imaging. During an imaging session, worms were partially covered if not being immediately prepared for an experiment. Worms were imaged at roughly 70–140 uW/mm 2  (measured at sample plane) of light at the sample, for 20–30 ms exposures within an 80 ms duty cycle – 20–30 ms of light exposure and 50–60 ms of blank time. Optogenetic illumination was performed using a digital micromirror device (Mightex Polygon P1000) combined with an LDI-7 laser combiner (89North)) at 640 nm, powered to 10–20% of maximum laser intensity. Illumination was performed in widefield for worms with optogenetic construct expressed under a single-neuron promoter, and localized to 5×5 um for selective single-neuron illumination of optogenetic constructs driven by multi-neuronal promoters. Fictive behavioral decoding of whole-brain recordings was performed as previously described. 22 Identification of neuron identities In each recording, we detected 45–140 neurons. The strains used for experiments here express the NeuroPAL genetic cassette, 48  which uses a genetically defined combination of 4 fluorophores with various neuronal expression patterns to discriminate neurons based on multi-color reporter expression. Neurons were identified by assessing their NeuroPAL colors, anatomical position, relationship to surrounding neurons, and their activity patterns, as established by community-sourced datasets. 49  Neuron labels were assigned by hand. In many cases ambiguity still existed, so here we opted for a conservative approach and chose not to ID neurons which could not be identified beyond reasonable doubt. In some cases, ambiguous identities are denoted in parentheses. QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS Linear predictive modeling framework For predictive modeling, we used KNN and SVM classifiers, respectively, implemented by scikit-learn, 50  with 20-fold cross-validation to limit overfitting. To account for class imbalance, we report the balanced accuracy metric, which is equivalent to the accuracy score with class-balanced sample weights. Model performance was averaged over 100 iterations with randomized seeds for cross-validation. For timepoint predictive analysis, separate models were fit on neural activity (ΔF/F and its instantaneous derivative as independent features) at single timepoints relative to stimulation. In both cases, control distributions were generated by shuffling class labels. Region of interest (ROI) detection and time series extraction from volumetric Ca2+ imaging data ROI detection from neural time series videos was adapted from previous studies, 22  and implemented by the Napari 51 
 eats-worm  plugin. Briefly, interframe motion was first registered using manual keyframe tracking. 52  A reference ROI movie was then generated composed of each image plane by averaging successive blocks of 20–200 movie frames to reduce noise. Each frame of the reference was adaptively thresholded based on median image brightness, median filtered, then convolved with a gaussian kernel. Local maxima were found and merged if peaks were adjacent within a greedy threshold. For each ROI center, a surrounding region with radius 5–7 was defined, with overlapping adjacent regions excluded via Voronoi tessellation with area shrinkage of 0.5 pixels. ROIs in adjacent timepoints were linked via local greedy matching. Cells below detection threshold were extrapolated based on the motion of neighboring ROIs. Finally, time-varying multi-plane ROIs were adjoined based on overlap. Each neuron was manually inspected for artifacts and overlapping fluorescence with adjacent neurons.. For high temporal resolution half-brain imaging experiments, images were compressed along the Z axis via maximum intensity projection, becoming 3D ROIs. Neural time series extraction was also adapted from previous studies. 22  Briefly, for each 4D ROI, a single-cell fluorescence intensity was computed taking the average of the brightest 30–60 voxels at every time point after subtracting z-plane specific background intensity. Background values were computed by averaging pixels not belonging to any ROI within a radius of 21 pixels. ΔF/F0 was computed for each neuron, with F0 as mean background fluorescence. Neural time series derivatives and embeddings Derivatives and PCA on neural time series data was performed as previously described 22 , 53 . Specifically, total-variation regularization was used to compute de-noised time derivatives while resolving the accuracy of command state transitions to single frames. Differentiation provides a degree of stationarity to the signal, improving subsequent analyses. This approach was also used for the calculation of proj(headswing). In more detail, for calculating proj(headswing), we first subtracted the sums of projections to the derivative time series onto temporal PC1. Next, we sub-segmented forward command states and remove unsustained forward states defined by a threshold (<4s). The resulting segments were concatenated prior to PCA 54  to compute loadings. Full time series are then projected onto these loadings. Prior to PCA, time series were detrended and regularized. For comparison across animals, we applied PC matching by inverting projections, if necessary, such that the genetically identified neuron SMDV would be negative. In some cases, such as a large number of neurons drifting out of the focal plane contributing high variance, this procedure was adjusted by first filtering neurons with thresholded (0.3–0.5) normalized covariance to SMDV or by cropping the beginning, or end, of the full time series. Correlation to linear projection analysis To examine how individual neuron activity is reflected in the command state or headswing projections, we performed correlation analysis. First we computed command state and headswing linear projections as described above. Individual neuron traces were then smoothened and linearly detrended and correlated to each projection using the Pearson correlation coefficient. In the case of the headswing projection comparisons, trace derivatives and a nonlinear normalization procedure was first applied by scaling positive and negative ranges to +1 and −1 respectively. This balances the amplitude structure of sinusoid-like neurons, reducing long timescale drift and calcium imaging floor effects from (+) or (−) embedding neurons.  Figures 2D  and  2F  exclude neurons with absolute correlation coefficient < 0.1 for the command state projection, and <0.05 for the headswing projection. Evaluating the effect of neuron stimulation on global state transition The significance of the effect of neuron stimulation on command state transitions (time to REV initiation or REV termination) was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The comparable control distribution is built from spontaneous command state transitions unaffected by stimulation. To calculate control null distribution, first we took unstimulated states from control worms and computed the marginal distribution of time until the next state transition. We then accumulated shifted state durations selected from our experimental group, based on time from stimulation, to build the marginal distribution of time until state transition. Proj(command) ramping and phase analysis The time constant of proj(command) ramping was calculated by fitting a single exponential curve to proj(command) traces scaled to each reversal. proj(command) and proj(headswing) phase were calculated by smoothing both traces with a gaussian filter, identifying extrema (scipy.signal.find_peaks), and linearly interpolating between peaks and valleys. The ratio of proj(command) to proj(headswing) period was calculated using the inter-peak-intervals of each signal for the corresponding cycles. Sinusoid fitting For fitting sinusoids to headswing-associated neurons, we selected a subset of neurons with high correlation to proj(headswing) to fit on: SMDVL, SMDVR, SMDDL, SMDDR, RIVL, RIVR, and additionally proj(headswing) and fit the following functional form: A sin(ωt + φ), where A is a vector of neuron weights, ω is the frequency of the sine, and φ is the phase offset. Parameters were fit by Nelder-Mead optimization using the python package  lmfit . Minimum and maximum constraints were placed on fit parameters depending on the parameter being evaluated; for example, when evaluating ω or φ, dorsal and ventral head curvature associated neurons were constrained to have non-zero amplitudes of opposite sign and curves were normalized to the interval {−1, 1}. We further conducted these analyses on trials with and without AWA stimulation. In all cases, we fit on the numerical derivative of neuron curves, estimated using total-variance regularization, 53  and these curves were then smoothed with a box filter. We found our results were similar without temporal smoothing. For parameter initial values, ω = 0.18, φ = π, and amplitudes were as follows: proj(headswing), SMDDL, and SMDDR: 1, SMDVL, SMDVR: −1, RIVL, RIVR: −0.5.
